Assumption corrected

Inspectors only look at the current plan

Daniel had been building against an IRS-shaped threat model: keep every past version, because an inspector may one day ask to compare against the plan from four or five years ago. Julio said that is not how Coast Guard inspections work.

Julio  An inspection assesses the current CSP and nothing else. The only records they reach back for are exercise records, retained two to three years. "Let me see your plan from five years ago" does not happen.

This does not kill plan_version, but it does move the justification. Immutable frozen versions still earn their keep for amendment diffing, the five-year renewal, and replaying a surveillance run against a ruleset that has since changed. They are not an audit-lookback requirement. Anything in the roadmap that was priced as inspector-driven retention should be repriced.

The hard problem

Where the SSI lives

Everyone agreed LLMs cannot read CSPs directly and that ingesting a plan written by another provider stays manual for now. Nobody agreed on where the data should sit. Three positions, none of them resolved on the call.

Julio

Hosted portal

Clients log in, see their facility and their data. We hold backend visibility across every MTSA-regulated client, so a regulation change gets run once and everyone affected gets an alert explaining what moved. His precedent: Homeport already accepts plan uploads and SSI, so a hosted model is not automatically disqualified.

Austin

Engine and boxcar

How MSRAM was actually built. We own the engine, the calculation logic. The customer owns the boxcar, their data file. They attach it locally, process, save, submit. We never hold the sensitive data. Control the engine with a redistribution agreement and a registration key tied to the install, while accepting that people will share it anyway.

Justin

Reduced-fidelity profile

Keep only a generic client profile server-side, enough to know which clients an update touches and to notify them, without their granular data. He also flagged that holding any of it likely means formally designating a system and segmenting that data.

Three surfaces

Authoring, audit, surveillance

Daniel described the engine as the beating heart driving three products. The pipeline briefing already made the case that they are one evaluation engine invoked differently. What the call added was detail on the two that are not the plan builder.

SurfaceWhat it doesConstraint raised
Authoring The interview, then generation. Blocks export while required items are missing; the example given was an unanswered on-call protocol, which throws rather than producing a partial plan. Dropdowns keep answers structured, free text gets messy quick. Unresolved.
Audit Julio's idea. Audit any plan, including ones we did not write. Auditor walks a checklist, clicks a deficiency, gets the CFR citation attached to it plus pre-written remediation language and a free text field. Two triggers: routine annual review, or a Coast Guard finding. Plans are SSI, so the first version is manual.
Surveillance Named outright as the recurring revenue stream. Holds the plan, watches for regulation changes and new vulnerabilities, tells the client which specific point of their CSP just stopped being compliant. Turnaround after a reg change is unknown. Sales cannot promise it.

Say these out loud

Two ways surveillance goes wrong

Selling instant turnaround. Daniel The regulation changes and twenty minutes later the client gets an email is not a promise we can keep. Getting a rule change through the ruleset takes as long as it takes, and nobody knows that number yet. Salespeople need to say shortly after and leave the slack in.

Crying wolf. Daniel A generic go look at your CSP alert trains clients to ignore alerts. Every notification has to name the specific thing that moved. Julio's version of the same point is the useful one: key the alert to the client's own critical asset list, so it reads as reconsider the criticality of this asset, or a new vulnerability now affects an asset you listed.

Already built

What we are not writing from scratch

Four things came up as existing assets rather than new work.

SARA and SAM Carry over roughly as-is minus the compliance dropdown. They are RMF-shaped, so MTSA requirements get added and things that do not apply come out; STIGs were the example. SAM already has a selectable report-profile mechanism, which is the hook a compliance profile hangs on.
CFDD questionnaire Exposure and integrity questions, an algorithm assigning qualitative values, aggregated into a per-asset criticality rating. Daniel wants more questions and a scoring pass so like items rate alike. Justin fold it into data ingestion as its own tab rather than shipping it as a standalone deliverable people scroll away to.
Coast Guard inspector job aid The published checklist inspectors will actually use on CSPs. Basic, high level, non-technical, structured like the existing physical security one, and subject to change. Justin break its items into applicability branching so a user is told this applies, this does not.
Nessus ingest Feeds the per-asset vulnerability register. Each finding gets an answer: patched or fixed, who fixed it, what the resolution was. Findings that cannot be resolved capture compensating controls and a date to re-look. Daniel's read of the regulation is that unresolved and unexplained does not pass.

The business model

Borrow the structure from oil spill response

Julio's argument, and the part of the call that had the most conviction behind it. Do not invent a pricing model. Copy the one the maritime industry already understands and already budgets for.

Julio  An oil spill response contract is an annual fee covering plan maintenance, updates, and recurrent training, with separate published rates for actual response. The industry is already used to it. We just have to work out what the pricing looks like.

His read on timing: cyber today is where oil spill response was before it was regulated. Incidents are happening all day, every day, and response is where the revenue is. His prediction is that the Coast Guard eventually requires a contracted cyber response team on retainer, able to act within a set time, the same way it requires oil spill response contracts. Sell ahead of that.

TierShape
One-off Build the plan, hand it over, they deal with it. Julio write the first Coast Guard inspection into the contract, so the deliverable is the plan plus support through the inspection that proves it holds.
Self-service They hold the tool and update things themselves. This is the tier the boxcar architecture serves cleanly.
